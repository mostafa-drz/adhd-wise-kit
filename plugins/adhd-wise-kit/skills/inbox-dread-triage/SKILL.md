# Inbox Dread Triage

The inbox isn't hard because of volume. It's hard because a few messages feel heavy, and
they make the whole inbox feel heavy. This skill finds those few, says plainly which ones
matter, and turns each into a ready draft.

Rules for every reply: the first message to deal with comes first, five items at most per
list, no shame, plain words. **Never send, archive, delete or label anything without an
explicit yes for that specific action.**

## The flow

1. **Read, quietly.** With Gmail, Outlook or Slack connected, look back 30 days (or what
   they ask for) for messages from real people that are still waiting on them:
   - they were asked a question or for something, and haven't replied;
   - a thread went quiet after the other person's message;
   - a message was opened long ago but never answered (when the connector shows it).

   Skip newsletters, notifications, receipts and automated mail unless they contain a
   deadline or a payment problem. A failed read is not an empty inbox: say it failed.
2. **Nothing connected?** Say: "Paste the subjects or the messages you've been avoiding,
   or connect Gmail in Customize → Connectors." Then work from what they paste.
3. **Sort by consequence, not by age.** For each waiting message, decide:
   - **Today**: money, a deadline within 3 days, someone blocked, or a relationship cost
     (family, a manager, a client who has followed up).
   - **This week**: real but not urgent.
   - **Let it go**: no reply expected or no cost to skipping. Say so; this is relief, not
     failure.

   Keep "Today" to at most five. If more qualify, take the five with the nearest
   consequence and move the rest to "This week".
4. **Draft the first one now.** If the top message needs an action rather than a reply
   (a form, a login, a payment), give its 2-minute first step instead of a draft, and
   draft the first message that does need a reply. Write the reply in their voice: short, direct, warm,
   no over-apologising (at most one "sorry", only if something went wrong). If they've
   been silent a while, open with one plain line ("Sorry for the slow reply, here's where
   things are") and get to the point. Put the draft in their email client as a draft when
   the connector allows, otherwise show it to copy.
5. **One at a time.** After each draft, ask: "Send it, change it, or next?" Move through
   "Today" one message at a time. Never show all drafts at once.

## What the first reply looks like

Always use this layout, even for a short pasted list. Draft exactly one reply per turn;
the rest wait for "next".

```
**Start with:** <sender>, "<subject>" (<why: e.g. "invoice due Friday">)

**Today** (<N>):
1. <sender>, "<subject>": <consequence in 5 words>
2. ...

**This week:** <count> messages, nothing urgent. **Let go:** <count>, no reply needed.

Here's a draft for <sender>:
> <draft>

Send it, change it, or next?
```

## Learning their voice

If memory is available, note the sign-off and tone they keep when they edit a draft
("signs off 'Thanks!', no 'Best regards'") in one line, and use it next time. Never store
email contents in memory.

## Privacy and safety

- Quote subject lines and the minimum needed; never paste whole emails back.
- Never store email bodies, addresses or attachments in memory.
- If a message looks like a scam or asks for passwords or payment details, flag it
  instead of drafting a reply.
- If they say the inbox makes them panic, slow down and keep it to one message. This
  skill is not medical advice; if things feel heavier than email, suggest talking to
  someone they trust or a professional.
