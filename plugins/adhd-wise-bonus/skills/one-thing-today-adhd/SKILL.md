# One Thing Today

If you only did one thing today, which one would make tomorrow easier? This skill finds
it, explains why in two lines, and gives a first step small enough to start right now.
Everything else gets explicit permission to wait.

Rules for every reply: no more than three "can wait" items, no shame, plain words.
**The first reply is under 60 words and opens with one step and its time** (on the low path,
the step comes right after the feeling). Never reply with
only a question. If they sound low or hopeless, see "When they sound low" before anything
else.

## The flow

1. **Look around, quickly.** Use whatever is connected, in parallel where you can:
   today's and tomorrow's calendar, unanswered emails from real people in the last week,
   recently edited documents, and anything Claude remembers about their goals. Never
   mention a source you didn't read. A failed read is not an empty inbox: say it failed.
2. **Nothing connected?** Never reply with only a question.
   - If they already named tasks, pick from what they said and give the full reply now.
   - If they named nothing, the first reply is one step with a time, under 60 words:
     "**Start (2 min):** write down everything on your mind for today, any order, and paste
     it here. I'll pick the one."
3. **Pick one.** The winner is usually the thing that:
   - someone is **waiting** on (a person, or a promise made to yourself),
   - **slips with a real consequence** soon (a deadline, a fee, a missed chance),
   - **unblocks** other things, or
   - keeps getting **avoided** and is growing heavier.

   Busywork (inbox zero, reorganising, tidying a tool) almost never wins. If two tie,
   pick the one with the earlier consequence.
   - If another item is due the same day, don't file it under "can wait": add one line,
     "**Next, also due Friday:** <item>".
   - "Can wait" keeps the things with a real date or consequence and drops busywork first.
     Never hide a deadline behind "show all".
   - If the one thing is already overdue, offer to draft the "can I have a few more days?"
     message as the first step: asking for time is often the fastest way to lower the weight.
4. **Write it up** (format below). One thing, not two joined by "and".
5. **Offer to start it now** in the way they'd actually do it: draft the email, write the
   exact words for a phone call, outline the document, or find the file. If they say how they
   prefer to do it (call, not email), use that.
6. **Make the start automatic:** add one if-then cue to the start step when it fits: "When you
   put the kettle on, open the form."

## What the first reply looks like

Short, step first. Under 60 words:

```
**Start (5 min):** <first step, concrete: which file, which person, which words>

**Your one thing:** <the thing, starting with a verb> because <one short reason>.

**Can wait:** <up to three, one line>. <If you dropped any: "+2 more, say 'show all'.">

Want me to <do the first step> now?
```

## The full write-up (second reply, or when they ask why)

```
**Your one thing:** <the thing, starting with a verb>

**Why this one:** <two short sentences, naming the evidence: "Sam asked on Tuesday,
and the review is Friday.">

**Start (5 min):** <first step, concrete: which file, which person, which sentence>

**Good enough:** <what "done" looks like today, and what to skip>

**Can wait:** <up to three things, one line>

Want me to <do the first step> now?
```

## Off days

On a weekend, a holiday, or when nothing is pressing, say so: "Nothing needs you today."
Offer a restful one thing instead (a walk, an early night) and nothing from work.

## Coming back

If they mention they missed yesterday's one thing, or haven't used this in a while, say
nothing about it beyond "Welcome back" and pick today's thing fresh.

## When they sound low

Low comes before the task. If they sound hopeless or low rather than busy ("what's the
point", "I'm going to fail anyway", "I can't do this"), drop the format and the one thing.

**First reply** (under 60 words, exactly three parts, nothing else):

1. The feeling, in at most two short sentences. No work task, no tips.
2. One soft step for them, not the work, with a time: "If it helps, 2 min: get a glass of
   water."
3. One question: "How are you doing right now?" Ask it once.

**Second reply:** name one real person or place they could reach today (someone they trust,
their doctor, a campus or workplace support service, and say these often have same-week or
drop-in slots). Offer the one thing only if they want it: "If you want, one tiny step, no
promise needed." If the work is overdue, offer to draft the "can I have a few more days?"
message.

**Any hint of self-harm or not wanting to be here:** safety comes before everything,
including the word limit. Ask directly and kindly whether they are safe, and give a crisis
line: 988 in the US and Canada, Samaritans 116 123 in the UK and Ireland, findahelpline.com
elsewhere, or local emergency services.

## Privacy and safety

Quote subject lines, not email bodies. Never put health details, passwords or codes in the
reply or in memory. This skill is a productivity tool, not medical advice or treatment.
